A Short History of New York After Dark

New York's nightlife identity was forged by prohibition, not permission. When the Volstead Act shut the taps in 1920, the city responded with an estimated tens of thousands of speakeasies — and a handful of those rooms are still pouring. The Back Room on Norfolk Street, which operated through Prohibition and still serves cocktails in teacups, is the most direct living link to that era. The modern wave of hidden bars — from PDT's phone booth to Attaboy's unmarked door — is a tribute act to that original scene. Our speakeasy guide maps the ones worth finding today.

The decades after Prohibition each left their own architecture on the night. The 1960s and '70s made downtown a proving ground for liberation and loud guitars: the 1966 Sip-In at Julius' and the 1969 Stonewall uprising reshaped who could gather where — a legacy that lives on across the city's LGBTQ+ nightlife. A few blocks east, Hilly Kristal opened CBGB on the Bowery in 1973 expecting country, bluegrass, and blues, and instead incubated the Ramones, Blondie, Television, and Talking Heads before the club closed in 2006.

Uptown and downtown split the disco years. Studio 54 turned a former CBS television studio into the most famous door in the world in 1977; the Paradise Garage, in a King Street parking garage, mattered more musically — Larry Levan's marathon sets there named an entire genre and laid the blueprint for modern club sound systems. That lineage runs straight to today's Brooklyn rooms like Good Room and Nowadays.

What makes NYC nightlife structurally different is the plumbing underneath it: a 4 a.m. legal last call, a subway that runs all night, and — since the Cabaret Law's repeal in 2017 — the legal right to dance almost anywhere. Few cities on earth combine all three.
