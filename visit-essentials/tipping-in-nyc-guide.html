Tipping in NYC: How Much, Where, and When to Skip It

Tipping in NYC is simpler than the internet makes it sound: roughly 20% at sit-down restaurants, $1–2 per drink at bars, and a careful look at any check that already says “gratuity included.” This guide covers every situation you’ll actually hit on a night out — with current numbers, not folklore.

One piece of context makes all of it make sense: tipped workers in New York are paid a reduced base wage ($10.65/hour for food service workers in 2026) on the legal assumption that tips close the gap. That’s why table-service tipping here is effectively mandatory while counter-service tipping genuinely isn’t. Last verified: 2026-08-26.

Restaurants: 18–22%, and What the Fine Print Means

Twenty percent is the standard at any table-service restaurant in New York, from a red-sauce joint to a tasting menu. Multiple 2025–2026 tipping guides converge on the same range: 18% is the floor, 20% is normal, 22%+ signals great service or a high-end room. If the service was genuinely bad, say something to a manager rather than silently tipping 10% — the server may not even control what went wrong.

Automatic gratuity for groups

Under NYC consumer protection rules, a restaurant may add a mandatory gratuity for large parties only if the charge is conspicuously disclosed before you order — on the menu or posted signage. In practice most places apply it to parties of 6 or 8 or more, at 18–20%. When it’s on the check, your tip is handled; don’t add another 20% on the card screen out of reflex. “Service charges” that aren’t tips

A few restaurants use flat “service,” “administrative,” or “wellness” charges. NYC rules require these surcharges to be disclosed on the menu, but disclosure doesn’t tell you where the money goes — an administrative fee may not reach your server at all. If the line item doesn’t literally say “gratuity,” it’s reasonable to ask, “Does this go to the staff?” Servers answer that question honestly and constantly.

Bars, Cocktail Lounges, and Clubs

Ordering at the bar

The classic move: $1 per beer or shot, $2 per cocktail, dropped on the bar in cash. On a running tab, tip 20% at close like a restaurant check. At serious cocktail bars where each drink takes three minutes of labor, $2 per drink or 20% is the norm — and tipping well on round one is the cheapest queue-jump in the city. If it’s your first night out here, our first-timer’s guide to NYC nightlife covers the rest of the etiquette.

Bottle service: read the check before you tip

Almost every NYC club adds gratuity to bottle-service bills automatically — typically 18–20%, sometimes with a separate 5–10% “administrative” or venue fee on top. Nightlife booking guides consistently advise budgeting 25–35% over the quoted table minimum for tax and tip combined. Two rules protect you: first, find the gratuity line before adding anything on the payment screen, because double-tipping a $2,000 bill is a $400 mistake; second, know that admin fees are not necessarily staff tips — if you want your server specifically taken care of beyond the auto-gratuity, hand them cash. Coat check, doormen, bathroom attendants

Coat check runs $1–2 per item in cash, handed over at pickup; if the venue charges a mandatory coat fee, that’s revenue, not a tip, and a dollar for the person actually fetching your coat is still a kind move. Club doormen don’t expect tips for simply doing their job, and waving cash at a door rarely works the way movies suggest. Bathroom attendants: $1 if you use the towel or the mints.

Getting Around and Getting Home

Yellow-cab payment screens famously default to 20/25/30% suggestions — a nudge design documented by Stanford and other researchers to raise average tips. You’re not obligated to pick a button: 15–20% is a normal cab tip, and there’s a custom-amount option. For Uber and Lyft, 10–20% or a flat $2–5 in the app is standard; nobody tips the subway. Speaking of which, the train is the honest late-night budget play — our subway-for-nightlife guide covers tapping in with OMNY at 3am. Hotel-wise: $2–5 per night for housekeeping (left daily, since staff rotate), $2–3 per bag for bellhops, and $5–20 for a concierge who pulls off something genuinely hard, like a same-night table.

Tip Screens and the Counter-Service Question

The flipped iPad asking 18/20/25% for a bottle of water is real, and you’re allowed to press “no tip” without shame. Here’s the honest framework New Yorkers actually use. Someone made or served you something — a barista pulling espresso, a counter cook assembling your late-night order — and a dollar or 10% is a decent gesture, though still optional. Nobody performed a service beyond ringing you up — a bodega bottle of Gatorade, a merch stand, a self-checkout screen that asks for a tip anyway — and $0 is completely normal. Counter tips are not wage-subsidy tips: unlike servers, counter staff at most quick-service spots earn the full minimum wage. The screens are engineered awkwardness, not obligation. One practical note: carry $20–30 in singles and fives on a night out. Coat check, per-round bar tips, and doormen all run on small bills, and cash tips reach staff same-night. FAQ

Is tipping optional in New York City?

At sit-down restaurants and bars, no — not in any practical sense. Tipped workers in New York are paid a lower base wage ($10.65/hour for food service in 2026) on the assumption that tips make up the difference. Skipping the tip at table service is read as a statement, not a choice. Counter service and grab-and-go are the exception: tipping there is genuinely optional.

Do I tip on the pre-tax or post-tax total?

Etiquette guides say pre-tax is acceptable; most New Yorkers tip on the full total because the math is easier. On a $100 check the difference is about $1.80. Either is fine — just don’t use tax math as a way to shave the tip below 18%.

Do I still tip if the check says gratuity or service charge is included?

No double tip is expected. If an 18–20% gratuity or service charge is already on the bill — common for parties of 6–8+ and near-universal for bottle service — you’re done, though you can add a few dollars for exceptional service. Read the check before adding a tip on the payment screen, because the screen won’t stop you from tipping twice.

Is it better to tip in cash or on the card?

Servers and bartenders generally prefer cash: they take it home that night and it isn’t subject to card-processing deductions some venues pass along. A card tip is completely acceptable everywhere, so don’t stress about it — but a stack of small bills is the smoothest way to handle bartenders, coat check, and doormen on a night out.
