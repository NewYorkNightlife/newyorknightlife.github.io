Money Exchange in NYC: The Honest Guide

Here’s the answer most exchange counters hope you never hear: don’t exchange cash at the airport or in Times Square, because the spread quietly takes 5–15% of your money. Use an ATM with the right card, carry a modest amount of cash, and pay for everything else by tap.

This page covers where the bad deals hide, the cheapest way to get dollars, how cashless New York really is in 2026, and the short list of places where cash is still king. Numbers last verified: 2026-08-26.

Why Airport Kiosks and Tourist-Strip Counters Are a Bad Deal

Exchange counters rarely charge an honest-looking fee. Instead, the cost lives in the exchange rate itself: consumer-finance analyses in 2025 put typical airport-kiosk spreads at 5–10% worse than the mid-market rate, with several reviews finding traditional kiosks running 8–10% premiums and worst cases in the 10–15% range once flat transaction fees ($5–15) are stacked on top. On $500, that’s $25–75 evaporating before you’ve left the terminal. Street-level storefronts around Times Square work the same way — a big “NO COMMISSION” sign usually means the commission is hidden in the rate. The test is simple: pull up the mid-market rate on your phone (any search engine will show it) and compare. If the counter’s rate is more than 2–3% off, walk.

Currency exchange isn’t the only rate game aimed at visitors — the broader catalog lives in our NYC tourist scams guide.

The Cheap Way: An ATM and the Right Card

For international visitors, the consistently cheapest dollars come from a bank ATM using a debit card with no foreign-transaction fee. The ATM gives you close to the wholesale rate; your only costs are your bank’s policy and the machine’s surcharge. Three rules keep those near zero:

  • Use ATMs attached to real banks (Chase, Citi, Bank of America, Capital One branches are everywhere in Manhattan) — not the freestanding machines in delis and club vestibules, which charge the highest surcharges and are occasionally tampered with.
  • Know your card’s fees before you fly. Many banks charge 1–3% on foreign withdrawals plus a flat fee; travel-friendly accounts charge nothing and some refund ATM surcharges. Bankrate’s 2025 study puts the average out-of-network ATM cost at a record $4.86 per withdrawal, so one larger withdrawal beats five small ones.
  • Always decline “conversion to your home currency” at the ATM or card terminal. That option — dynamic currency conversion — adds its own 3–12% markup. Choose to be charged in US dollars, every time.

Bringing some cash from home? Order dollars from your own bank before the trip — home-bank rates are usually far better than anything at JFK.

How Cashless Is NYC, Really?

Very. The subway and buses are now tap-to-pay only: OMNY readers take contactless cards and phones directly, and MetroCards were retired in 2026 (tap logistics for a night out are covered in our subway-for-nightlife guide). Taxis are required to accept cards. Practically every bar, club, and restaurant takes contactless, and plenty of counters have gone card-only. You can land at JFK with zero US cash and function fine for days — which is exactly why exchanging a fat stack of bills in advance is unnecessary.

The cash-only holdouts

And yet. A stubborn, wonderful layer of the city still runs on paper:

  • Dollar-slice joints and food carts — the late-night food economy leans cash (many halal carts now take cards, but never all, and never the one you’re standing at). Plan the 3am eat with our late-night food map.
  • Chinatown classics — Great NY Noodletown (open to 4am, an institution) is cash-only, as are neighbors like Shu Jiao Fu Zhou.
  • Some dive bars — a shrinking but real breed keeps a cash register and an ATM in the corner charging $3.50 for the privilege.
  • Cash-preferred legends — a handful of famous restaurants still don’t take standard credit cards; check before you go rather than assuming.

If You Truly Must Exchange Cash

Sometimes you land with euros, pounds, or yen that need to become dollars today. Rank your options: a dedicated exchange office with posted rates — Currency Exchange International (CXI) operates Manhattan locations and publishes rates online so you can check before walking in — then a major bank branch (Citibank exchanges for non-customers at many branches, including Midtown; Chase policies vary by branch and favor account holders). Hotel front desks and street kiosks come last, and “last” is generous. Whatever the counter, ask one question: “After all fees, how many dollars do I receive for this amount?” Then divide and compare with the mid-market rate on your phone. Exchanging money with anyone who approaches you on the street isn’t an exchange; it’s a magic trick where your money disappears.

Counterfeit awareness

Bartenders check $50s and $100s for a reason. Get your cash from bank ATMs, prefer $20s (accepted everywhere, rarely faked, never sneered at), and on any large bill you receive in change, look for the embedded security strip and the color-shifting ink in the corner numeral. If a bill feels waxy or the paper is wrong, hand it back.

The cash tipping advantage

One more reason to keep bills on you: cash tips go home with staff that night, while card tips arrive on payroll, sometimes minus processing deductions. FAQ

How much cash should I carry on a night out in NYC?

$50–100 covers a typical night: small bills for bar tips and coat check, a cash-only slice at 3am, and a margin if a card reader dies. Carry more only if your plans include a known cash-only restaurant. Everything else — bars, clubs, taxis, the subway — takes cards.

Can I get by in NYC with no cash at all?

Almost. The subway is tap-to-pay only (OMNY), nearly all bars and restaurants take cards, and taxis accept cards by law. But dollar-slice joints, many food carts, some dive bars, and a handful of beloved restaurants like Great NY Noodletown remain cash-only, and cash tips reach staff faster. Zero cash works until it suddenly doesn’t — carry a little.

Where can I exchange foreign currency in Manhattan if I have to?

Dedicated exchange offices such as Currency Exchange International (CXI) and bank branches (Citibank and some Chase locations serve non-customers) generally beat street-level kiosks. Compare the offered rate against the mid-market rate on your phone before committing, and ask for the all-in amount you’ll receive — that one question exposes hidden fees.

Should I worry about counterfeit bills in NYC?

It’s a small risk, but real enough that bars check $50s and $100s with pens and lights. Protect yourself the same way: get cash from bank ATMs rather than street exchanges or strangers, prefer $20s, and glance for the security strip and color-shifting ink on big bills. If a deal on the street involves changing money, it’s a scam, full stop.
