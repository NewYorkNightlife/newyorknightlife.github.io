How NYC Club Doors Actually Work (And How to Get Past Them)

Most "how to get into clubs" advice is written about a Vegas that doesn't exist here. New York doors run on a few different systems, and the winning move depends entirely on which system you're facing. Below: the real door models, illustrated with verified policies from our venue data (checked 2026-08-25).

Know which door you're facing

The ticket door. The easiest system, and the one visitors most often miss. At Paragon in Bushwick, the official policy is that RSVP is required to enter — walk-up without one is explicitly not allowed. There is no charm strategy; buy the ticket on RA or Dice, where tiered presales are cheaper than the door. Sell-out risk is the only real obstacle, so buy early for headline nights.

The tiered big-room door. Nebula and Marquee sell GA tickets (roughly $20–60 at Nebula, ~$30–70 reported at Marquee) and layer VIP tables on top — Marquee even sells a "Table Share Experience" that includes admission at a shared table. Ticket in hand, your remaining problems are dress (upscale, enforced: collared shirts and dress shoes for men at Nebula, no athletic wear) and timing — both doors favor dressed-up groups arriving before midnight.

The discretionary door. Le Bain puts it in writing that there's no cover for standing room — entry is free, and entry is precisely the thing not guaranteed. The door is selective, gets harder after 11pm, and responds to dress (no sportswear, jerseys, or hats) and group composition. The insider route: weekend day sessions from 2pm, when the same rooftop is easy.

The reservation-is-the-door model. At The Box, reservations are effectively required — without one, entry is the doorman's call, and the venue's own stated formula is "vibe multiplied by style divided by how many people you're with." Tables reportedly run $500+ minimums. That formula, incidentally, is every discretionary door in one sentence.

The culture door. House of Yes has no formal dress code — instead, themed and costume dress is strongly encouraged ("anything that displays effort will do," per the venue). The door isn't filtering for money; it's filtering for participation. Buy the presale (tickets run roughly $0–40 and good nights sell out), commit to the theme, and you're in.

The strategy, compressed

  • Buy your way past uncertainty. If a venue sells presale tickets, that's the entire game — do that. The door line is for people who didn't.
  • Dress for the strictest room in your night. Collared shirt and real shoes get you into everything above except House of Yes, where a costume does.
  • Go early. Every selective door in our data hardens between 11pm and midnight. Arriving at 10:30 is the cheapest VIP upgrade in the city.
  • Mind the group. The Box's formula penalizes big groups explicitly; mixed, smaller groups fare better at every discretionary door.
  • Have a plan B. No strategy makes a discretionary door certain. A backup nearby (see the night planner) turns a rejection into a ten-minute detour.

What doesn't work

Arguing (doors have infinite leverage and no patience), flashing cash (that's what tables are for — book one honestly via the venue's own channels if you want that lane), and third-party "guaranteed entry" sellers, which range from overpriced to fake. And know when a door isn't the game at all: a huge share of great NYC nights — Nowadays, Knockdown Center, most of Bushwick — are ticket-and-ID doors where nobody is judging your shoes. If the exclusivity theater isn't fun for you, skip it; the music is often better on the other side of town anyway.
